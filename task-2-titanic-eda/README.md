For this task I did an exploratory analysis of the Titanic passenger data (the train.csv file, 891 passengers) in Python, using pandas, seaborn and matplotlib in a Jupyter notebook. The notebook is titanic_eda.ipynb. I wanted to find out who survived and what separated them from the people who didn't.

The data had some missing values, so I cleaned it first. 177 ages were missing, and since age differs a lot between ticket classes (the median is 37 in first class, 29 in second and 24 in third), I filled each missing age with the median for that passenger's class. Two passengers were missing where they boarded, so I filled those with the most common port, Southampton. The cabin column was 77% empty, so instead of inventing cabin numbers I replaced it with a yes/no column for whether a cabin was recorded. I also changed the sex and port columns to categories.

About 38.4% of passengers survived. Sex made the biggest difference: 74.2% of women survived against 18.9% of men. Class mattered too, with 63.0% survival in first class, 47.3% in second and 24.2% in third. Looking at both together, first-class women survived at 96.8% and third-class men at only 13.5%, and third-class women (50%) did better than first-class men (36.9%). The age chart shows most of the youngest children survived, and the correlation heatmap shows being female has the strongest link with survival (0.54), followed by ticket class, having a cabin recorded and fare.

Some limits: these patterns show who survived, not why. About a fifth of the ages were filled in by me, which creates a spike around age 24 in the age chart that isn't a real pattern. Having a cabin recorded is strongly tied to class, so it mostly reflects first-class passengers. And some groups are small, so their rates are less certain.

The dataset isn't stored in this repo. I got train.csv from the Kaggle competition "Titanic: Machine Learning from Disaster".
